# 3. Natural-Language Rule Construction

## 3.1 Rule Construction by Natural Language

In this study, rules are constructed through natural-language interaction rather than through direct modification of model parameters, platform-specific code, or external software enforcement. A rule may define an expected response, a contextual state, a role, a boundary, or a relationship between multiple contexts.

The central methodological assumption is deliberately limited: natural language can be used to *declare and organize* rules presented to an LLM. Whether those rules are recognized, maintained, or applied consistently is treated as an empirical question.

## 3.2 Boundary Declaration

A key component of the construction method is the declaration of conceptual boundaries. For example, a conversation may define two regions such as INSIDE and OUTSIDE and associate different rules with each region. The boundary itself is expressed in natural language rather than implemented as a separate software container.

Boundary declarations allow the study to test whether an LLM can distinguish between rule scopes and change the applicable rule when the declared context changes. The existence of such behavior does not by itself demonstrate a distinct internal mechanism; it provides observable behavior that can be compared across models and platforms.

## 3.3 Rule Modification and Dialogue

Rules need not remain fixed after their initial declaration. During exploratory construction, problems, ambiguities, or unintended behavior may lead to proposed modifications. A proposal may originate from the human participant or emerge as a suggestion generated by the AI during dialogue. The proposal can then be evaluated, accepted, rejected, or revised through subsequent interaction.

This dialogic process is treated here as a practical construction method rather than as evidence that the human and AI possess equivalent authority or understanding. Its relevant feature is that rule structures can develop iteratively through natural-language exchange rather than requiring a fully predetermined formal specification.

A simplified construction cycle is:

1. identify a problem or desired behavior;
2. propose a rule or structural modification;
3. evaluate the proposal through dialogue or testing;
4. adopt, reject, or revise the proposal;
5. observe the resulting behavior; and
6. repeat the process when new problems or possibilities appear.

## 3.4 Platform-Independent Construction

Because the construction method relies on natural-language declarations, the same rule description can in principle be presented to different LLM platforms without rewriting the rule as platform-specific software. This provides a common experimental input for cross-platform comparison.

However, common input does not imply identical implementation or identical behavior. Different models may interpret, represent, maintain, or apply the same natural-language rule in different ways. Accordingly, this study distinguishes between **construction portability** and **functional convergence**.

**Construction portability** means that the same or semantically equivalent natural-language rule specification can be presented across different platforms. **Functional convergence**, by contrast, concerns whether those platforms subsequently exhibit comparable rule-related functions, such as boundary recognition, contextual separation, switching, or rule-following.

The distinction can be summarized as follows:

> **The construction method may be shared across platforms; the resulting behavior or internal representation is not assumed to be identical.**

This distinction is central to the present research. Cross-platform similarity is therefore something to be measured rather than assumed, and differences between platforms are treated as research results rather than failures of the construction method.
