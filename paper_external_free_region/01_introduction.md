# 1. Introduction

Contemporary AI systems increasingly support tool use, web interaction, external applications, and multi-step task execution. However, these capabilities remain uneven across platforms. An operation available in one AI environment may be unavailable, restricted, or implemented differently in another. As a result, workflows designed around a specific platform can become difficult to transfer, reproduce, or extend when the underlying AI service changes.

A common response to this problem is to build additional software infrastructure around the AI system, including APIs, workflow engines, orchestration code, or platform-specific integrations. These approaches can be effective, but they also introduce implementation dependencies and may require users to adapt to predefined technical structures.

The Navi System explores a different starting point. Rather than beginning with executable functions, it begins with rules. Explicit natural-language meta-rules and construction rules are defined before agents, external actions, or service integrations are introduced. The intention is to establish the behavioral and safety boundaries of the environment before expanding its functional capabilities.

During the development of the Navi System, this rule-first construction process was extended from internally organized AI environments to externally organized environments. This led to the concept of an **External Free Region**: an externally placed, rule-governed environment in which designated agents or functions can be constructed and coordinated. The term *free* does not imply freedom from platform policies, legal constraints, service permissions, or human oversight. Rather, it refers to a user-defined construction space whose internal organization is governed by an explicit rule set while remaining subject to applicable external constraints.

Within such a region, an **external agent** is treated as an execution role positioned outside the originating AI's internal organization. Its behavior is determined by the rules of the externally constructed environment and by the capabilities and permissions actually available to it. Consequently, a function unavailable inside a particular AI platform does not become an internal capability of that AI. Instead, the broader system may gain access to that function through an externally placed execution component.

This distinction is central to the present study. The proposed framework does not seek to bypass platform restrictions or redefine the capabilities of an underlying model. It investigates whether **functional extension can be achieved through placement**: by relocating selected execution, memory, coordination, or integration functions into externally constructed, rule-governed structures.

Human readability is treated as a core design requirement. Active rules are maintained in a version-controlled repository, while a separate human-readable layer records explanations, proposed improvements, historical modifications, and unresolved issues. This allows users to inspect not only what rules currently apply, but also why those rules were introduced and how they evolved.

The present paper therefore asks whether a rule-first external construction method can extend the usable functions of heterogeneous AI systems while preserving explicit safety boundaries, human oversight, and traceable rule evolution. Rather than claiming universal portability, the study presents an exploratory architecture, initial observations, and a framework for systematic cross-platform evaluation.
